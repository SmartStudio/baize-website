When an AI product image on an e-commerce listing page does not match the item the buyer receives, the store operator bears the legal liability to the buyer. The store cannot shift it to the image software. It also cannot blame a gap between the designer and customer service. If a generated image only sets the background mood, the store labels it as AI-generated and adds real photos. Features that drive the purchase, such as color and pattern, cut and size, and accessories, must match the real item. Before listing, the product owner checks each one against the real item. When a buyer asks customer service whether the photos are real, the agent answers truthfully. If the image and the item differ clearly and the store did not disclose it, a court can find fraud. The store then refunds the buyer on return and pays three times the price as compensation.

## How a court ruled on a dispute over purely generated images

The Guangzhou Intermediate People's Court decided a clothing sales dispute caused by purely generated promotional images. The clothing store's listing page showed only AI-generated images. It had no real photos and no label saying the images were AI-generated. Before ordering, the buyer asked customer service whether the page images were real photos. The seller replied, "They are all photos of the real item." After delivery, the buyer found that the pattern, cut, and buttons were clearly different from the listing images.

The court held that under the E-Commerce Law and the Consumer Rights Protection Law, a business operator must disclose product information truthfully and accurately. The operator has a duty to review promotional content made with AI. The page must show the actual condition objectively and accurately, and must not use AI to invent product features. Generated content that differs clearly from the real item, without truthful disclosure, misleads the consumer into buying. That is fraud. The court ordered the seller to refund the buyer on return and pay three times the price as compensation. The court also said technology may be used to improve presentation, provided the seller gives consumers the necessary notice and explanation.

## Which details a generated image on a listing page must not change

The Measures for Labeling AI-Generated and Synthetic Content are in force. They require service providers to add a clear notice label to generated images that could confuse the public. E-commerce operations and design staff who publish generated or synthetic content must declare it and use the platform's labeling feature. They must not remove, alter, or hide the label. A designer who crops off the label corner while retouching, or a listing that goes up without a synthetic-content declaration, may break this rule. On October 16, 2026, Kuaishou E-commerce published a notice on a campaign against false advertising with AI material in apparel and footwear. It requires that "the product hero image (the first main image), SKU images, and product videos must show the real product." A listing that uses AI-generated effect images on the detail page must say so on that page and "clearly inform consumers." For how the legal team keeps a ledger when ad assets are produced in bulk, see <NoteLink slug="aigc-ad-assets-vs-legal-ledger">What Should a Legal Ledger Track for AI-Generated Ad Creatives?</NoteLink>.

When the design team builds a listing page, it should not apply one retouching standard to the whole long image. It splits the page into three parts and checks each separately: the scene background that sets the mood, the product itself that shows the workmanship, and the real photos kept as a baseline.

- **Areas where generation may assist**: outdoor streets and indoor mood, lighting and set design, or model pose references. Tools may generate these, with an AI-generated label on the image.
- **Features that must match the real item**: the product's color and pattern, its cut, its length and width, and its buttons and accessories. Buyers order based on these features. A generation tool must not redraw or invent them.
- **The real-photo minimum on every listing page**: however much synthetic background the mood images use, the page must include flat-lay photos and detail close-ups of the real item with no redrawing.

## How the product owner checks the real item before listing

A designer retouching images at a computer often has only the supplier's white-background images or design samples. The designer cannot touch the bulk goods that just arrived at the warehouse. Self-review by the design role alone cannot stop mismatches. Before a product link goes live, the sign-off for checking the real item belongs to the product owner or buyer. A company can configure its listing workflow so that a product link cannot be published without the real-item check signature.

The product owner takes one finished unit from the bulk-goods warehouse, the same as what ships to buyers. The owner places it next to the screen and compares it with each image on the listing page. The check focuses on three points. Did the retouching tool narrow the cut at the collar or cuffs? Was the fabric texture redrawn and distorted? Do the pattern position and button style match the unit in hand? If the factory changed the buttons or the stitching color in a bulk batch, the product owner first replaces the old images and shoots real photos of the new batch. The owner archives the check record with that period's bulk-goods sample photos.

## How customer service answers truthfully and what to do after a complaint

In that case, the court found the seller's answer "They are all photos of the real item" to be a false statement. When operations staff list a product, they also add to the customer service script library which images on that link are synthetic scenes and which are warehouse photos. Some tools can show an image-type tag for the product under discussion in the customer service sidebar. When a buyer asks in the chat window whether the photos are real, or worries about color differences, the agent follows the standard script. The agent states that the scene was generated with AI assistance and sends the buyer the real detail photos from the listing page to confirm.

As soon as the after-sales system receives a report that the goods do not match, or a mismatch complaint, the operations team takes down or replaces the disputed images. This keeps later visitors from being misled into ordering. Customer service and legal then arrange returns and refunds or compensation for existing buyers, under the relevant laws and platform rules. The product owner pulls the image review archive from before listing. The owner traces which generated image changed a feature of the real item and who signed it off, and closes the gap in retouching and review.

## AI product images: FAQ

### The supplier's images were AI-generated already. Is the store liable if it lists them as they are?

Yes. The business operator who sells the product is liable to consumers. A distributor or drop-shipping store that did not check the supplier's images item by item against the bulk goods before listing still bears the liability. If a mismatch dispute arises, the store first handles returns, exchanges, or compensation for the buyer as the law requires. Afterward it can seek recovery from the upstream supplier under the supply agreement.

### If we cut out a real photo of the garment and paste it onto an AI-generated street scene, does it count as a real photo?

The product itself was photographed, but the whole image is still a composite. Edge lighting and rendering can also change the garment's color. When publishing, the store still declares it and keeps the synthetic-content label. When asked, customer service explains that the background is a synthetic scene. The listing page also includes the original real photo without the cutout.

### The listing page says "images are for reference only." Is the store still liable for a mismatch?

If the difference is clear, the store may still be liable. In the clothing case the court decided, the listing page also had a notice that colors might differ slightly. The court examined whether the pattern, cut, and buttons in the images differed clearly from the real item, and whether the seller disclosed it truthfully. A general reference notice does not remove the legal liability for a clear mismatch.
