When a plant uses AI to watch non-production loads at night and on weekends for factory power anomalies, the alert only indicates which area and which time window exceeded the baseline and by how much. Who goes to the floor to check must be assigned in advance by sub-meter point. Energy anomalies at production line and workshop meter points belong to the workshop head; compressed air, cooling, lighting, and other public utility and auxiliary systems belong to the equipment supervisor; at night, the on-duty electrician goes to the floor first to verify. After checking, the responder fills in the cause and action taken on the alert ticket. Shutting down equipment follows the rules set by the equipment supervisor. AI does not remotely shut down equipment directly.

## Where nighttime idle load shows up

The Ministry of Industry and Information Technology's Industrial Power Demand-Side Management Work Guide lists energy anomaly analysis and energy-use behavior analysis among the functions of a power demand-side management platform, alongside fault diagnosis and location and incident early-warning alerts. The guide also notes that accurate and reliable data requires attention to metering instrument accuracy and calibration as well as system time synchronization. To investigate idle load during non-production hours, underlying meter collection and time synchronization must line up first.

A popular-science article on factory energy metering notes that sub-metering can be broken down level by level across the main incomer, workshops, production lines, key equipment, and auxiliary systems. Each meter point has a defined management purpose, and upper and lower statistical boundaries can be cross-checked against each other. Base load at night and on weekends after production stops is a common entry point for analysis: once a line has stopped, if power on a branch stays high, staff can trace down the sub-meters level by level to check for idling equipment, unclosed auxiliary systems, pipe network leaks, or unreasonable standby settings.

## Why a fixed threshold alone fails

Spotting energy anomalies during non-production hours cannot rely on drawing a single fixed power line in the backend. Product changeovers, high-power equipment startups, unscheduled night overtime, and equipment maintenance all cause normal load swings in a factory. Enforcing a rigid threshold without checking floor status will trigger a flood of alerts whenever an extra night shift runs. Calculating the power baseline requires factoring in output volume, shifts, product types, equipment utilization, and environmental conditions together. Meter disconnections, shutdowns, reboots, and missing data must be flagged first rather than mixed directly into normal statistics.

Some tools evaluate load level, change magnitude, and duration together when judging an anomaly, combining shift schedules, production work orders, and equipment running status to filter out normal fluctuations before routing the alert to a specific responsible area, so floor staff do not ignore alerts because of false alarms. ## Who checks after an alert fires

When data stays on a dashboard without an accountability mechanism, energy savings do not happen on the floor. In discussing electricity conservation, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology's Industrial Power Demand-Side Management Work Guide sets the sequence as "management first, process optimization second, retrofits third": tighten power distribution rules and floor management to cut waste first, optimize processes next, and retrofit equipment last. Routing nighttime alerts to specific posts is part of floor management.

The energy manager maps each sub-meter point to a responsible person in the backend, and alerts route by ownership when triggered:

- **Workshop and production line meter points**: checked by the workshop head during the day; at night, the on-duty team leader or on-duty electrician goes to the line first to check whether a machine was left on, an oven is still holding temperature, or a section is running unscheduled overtime.
- **Public utility and auxiliary system meter points**: compressed air stations, cooling towers, exhaust fans, and public lighting fall under the equipment supervisor; at night, the on-duty electrician inspects pipe network valves, leak points, and standby status.
- **Floor action and shutdown authority**: once the on-duty electrician identifies the situation, turning off lights or shutting down idling auxiliary machines follows standard operating procedures; anything involving main equipment or process thermal holding requires asking for approval under the rules set by the equipment supervisor before acting, and the system is not allowed to trip breakers remotely on its own.

## How to cut repeat alerts after checking

After floor staff check nighttime idle load, they write the actual cause on the alert ticket, distinguishing unscheduled workshop overtime, equipment left on after shift, compressed-air or water pipe leaks, and metering jumps caused by meter communication reconnects. The energy manager reviews the filled-in alert records weekly. If a production line triggers several alerts in a row that all turn out to be unscheduled overtime, the shift scheduling system and overtime work orders are connected into the judgment rules so the tool no longer flags night shifts backed by a work order.

Before-and-after power savings verification must compare nighttime base load on the same statistical basis to see whether unclosed equipment and pipe leaks actually dropped. ## Factory power anomalies: FAQ

### When AI spots nighttime idle load, can it remotely shut down equipment directly?

Do not let AI remotely trip breakers to shut down equipment. Power draw at night may come from a workshop running unscheduled work, equipment in a required thermal-holding or cooling phase, or staff performing maintenance on the floor. Cutting power remotely without checking the floor can harm equipment, work in progress, and personnel safety. The on-duty electrician should confirm status on site and handle it manually under the operating procedures set by the equipment supervisor.

### Can this work without sub-metering?

If a plant has only one main incoming meter, it can see only that total plant load is high at night, without pinpointing which workshop or public utility system is drawing power, leaving the on-duty electrician to walk the entire plant. A company can first install sub-meters on high-consumption workshops, energy-intensive key equipment, and auxiliary systems such as compressed air and refrigeration so upper and lower statistical boundaries can be cross-checked, then route nighttime alerts to specific responsible staff.

### What if there are too many energy anomaly alerts and nobody checks them?

First revise the energy anomaly detection rules: configure the tool to evaluate absolute power, change magnitude, and duration together rather than triggering on a single fixed threshold, and point each alert to a specific workshop or equipment zone. Next, connect production shift schedules, unscheduled overtime, and maintenance plans into the system to filter out false alerts caused by normal production swings. At the same time, require the on-duty person responsible to fill in the on-site verification result for every alert sent, and have the energy manager review the records regularly and tune the baseline.
