When AI sizes overseas warehouse replenishment, it only calculates a suggested quantity from a set formula and base tables. Whoever maintains a parameter or signs a document owns the stockout or overstock tied to it. The planner maintains the first-leg lead time, safety days, and forecasting method. Procurement checks and places the order. Orders over the company's own amount tier, first batches of new products, and promotion stock need the business lead's signature. In the post-review, the team separates missed data, wrong parameters, manual edits, and sudden market changes. Finance tracks inventory age and slow-moving stock value every month.

## How AI calculates a replenishment suggestion

Some cross-border e-commerce software uses two formulas for first-leg stock planning: a reorder point and a suggested replenishment quantity. When available stock in the overseas warehouse falls to the reorder point, the software raises a replenishment alert. The reorder point equals the first-leg lead time plus safety stock days, multiplied by average daily sales. For the suggested quantity, AI adds the first-leg transit time, safety days, and stocking cycle, and multiplies the sum by forecast daily sales to get total demand. It then subtracts current available stock in the overseas warehouse, stock in transit, and stock that has arrived but is not yet shelved. Some algorithms also subtract quantities that are ordered but not yet shipped.

Subtracting in-transit, pending-shelving, and ordered-but-unshipped stock prevents duplicate orders. The formula turns routine stocking from "it feels like we are about to run out" into a number someone can check. When AI drafts a replenishment plan, it lists the available stock, in-transit quantity, average daily sales, and cycle parameters it used, so upstream and downstream teams can check each item.

## Which parameters people set

The formula stays fixed. The accuracy of the result depends on the business parameters the planner or operations team maintains. People recalibrate the first-leg lead time, safety days, and forecasting method on a schedule against actual logistics and sales. Every parameter change is logged in the back end with who changed it, when, and why, so the tool does not keep running on outdated assumptions with nobody watching.

- **Full first-leg lead time**: The lead time covers the whole path from order to sellable in the warehouse. Use the average of the last few actual shipments and add a buffer. Peak-season ocean booking and warehouse inbound appointments take longer, so the planner raises the lead time ahead of time based on past peak seasons.
- **Rolling and promotion daily sales**: Average daily sales come from recent rolling data, with promotion seasons adjusted separately. If daily monitoring shows sales suddenly speeding up, replenish early. If sales slow, pause orders so you do not keep replenishing at the old pace and create slow-moving stock.
- **Different safety days by product**: Safety stock covers sales swings and first-leg delays, so products with large swings get more safety days. A stockout costs more than lost sales. It can also hurt marketplace ranking, so core products can have a more conservative reorder point. When the calculated quantity is too large, first shorten the stocking days, lower the safety days, or pause replenishment for long-tail products. ## Who signs the replenishment plan, and at what level

After AI drafts a replenishment plan, it does not send orders to the factory. Documents are signed by tier, separating routine replenishment from key stocking. For rolling replenishment of established products in a normal sales period, where a batch's value is within the company's own amount tier, the planner confirms the parameters, and procurement checks the lead time and confirms the order.

If a single purchase exceeds the company's own amount tier, or it is new product stock or promotion stock, the business lead signs. If the planner, operations, or the business lead raises or lowers the suggested quantity by hand during approval, the back end records the quantities before and after the edit and the reason, for later review.

## How to review stockouts and overstock

After goods arrive and sell, operations, procurement, and finance regularly look back at actual stockouts and overstock and break down the causes against the replenishment plan that was signed. The review pulls the inventory snapshot from the order date, the parameter log, and the signed approval form. It checks layer by layer: data maintenance, parameter calibration, manual edits, and market changes. The findings then feed back into the next round of parameters.

- **Errors in the base data**: If overseas warehouse book stock was not synced, an ocean shipment in transit was not entered, or ordered-but-unshipped quantity was not subtracted, and that caused over- or under-ordering, the role that maintains daily data and enters orders is responsible.
- **Stale or wrong parameters**: If the team knew the peak ocean booking season or a promotion was coming but kept the off-season lead time and old safety days, the planner is responsible.
- **Manual edit errors**: If AI calculated the suggestion from accurate data and reasonable parameters, and an approver added a large amount on a hunch and caused overstock, or cut the quantity hard and caused a stockout, the person who edited the number is responsible.
- **Sudden market changes**: If the data and parameters were accurate at order time, and after following the plan the business lead signed, demand in the destination market dropped sharply or marketplace rules changed, it counts as the business lead's business judgment. It is recorded and used to recalibrate the next round of forecast parameters.

Finance pulls the overseas warehouse inventory age table and slow-moving stock value every month. When aged stock in a product line crosses the company's own amount tier, finance lists it separately and sends it to the business lead to push clearance and tighten approvals for new products and promotions. ## Overseas warehouse replenishment: FAQ

### The formula's total replenishment quantity is too large and ties up too much cash. What should we do?

Do not order the full initial calculation as is. The planner and the business lead first sort products into tiers. Protect stock levels for core products first so they do not stock out and lose ranking. For other regular and slow-moving products, shorten the stocking cycle, lower the safety stock days, or pause purchasing of long-tail products, in that order, until the batch's cash commitment is back within the company's own amount tier.

### Ocean booking slows down in peak season. What goes wrong if we keep using the normal lead time?

The reorder point comes too late, and the overseas warehouse runs out while the goods are still at sea. The first-leg lead time covers the whole path from ordering production, through ocean booking, to the inbound appointment at the destination warehouse and the goods becoming sellable. Longer booking and inbound appointment times in peak season eat the buffer. Before peak season, the planner raises safety days based on past years and records the change.

### We ordered by the AI replenishment plan and ended up with overstock. Who is responsible?

Start from the snapshot saved with the replenishment plan and check the data and parameters. Missed in-transit stock or wrong inventory is the data maintenance role's error. Failing to adjust parameters for peak season or a slowdown is the planner's error. Adding quantity by hand mid-process is the error of whoever edited the number. If the data and parameters were accurate and the order followed the plan the business lead signed, a later market change falls under normal business judgment. Record it, adjust the safety days and forecasting model, and do not punish frontline staff based on the outcome.
